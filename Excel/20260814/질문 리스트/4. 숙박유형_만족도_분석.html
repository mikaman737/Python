Lodging style, satisfaction and behavioural intentions

Six research questions, tested and reported in publication format

One strong effect, one moderate, four nulls
The three outcome measures differ sharply from each other — a satisfaction–intention gap of large statistical significance. Gender predicts ratings modestly. Lodging style predicts nothing, and neither gender nor education predicts which lodging style a traveller chooses.

Research questions and verdicts

On your t-test idea. Comparing satisfaction against revisit intention is a good question, but it must be a paired (dependent-samples) t-test, not an independent-samples one. The same 751 travellers supplied both scores, so the observations are linked — an independent-samples test would ignore that pairing, inflate the standard error, and throw away most of the statistical power. I used paired t-tests plus a Wilcoxon signed-rank check and a repeated-measures ANOVA across all three measures. That turned out to be your strongest result.

RQ2 · Do the three measures differ from each other?

Repeated-measures comparison on the same travellers. Verdict: yes, decisively.

A clear descending ladder: travellers rate their satisfaction highest, their willingness to recommend lower, and their intention to revisit lowest of all.

Every pairwise difference is significant at p < .001 after Bonferroni correction, with effect sizes from dz = 0.34 to 0.56 — small to medium, and far larger than anything else in this dataset.

Substantively: being pleased with a trip does not translate into intending to repeat it. The 0.20-point drop from satisfaction to revisit intention is the single largest systematic pattern in the data.

Summary and interpretation

The pattern across all six questions is coherent. Situational and demographic variables predict ratings weakly or not at all — lodging style explains under 1% of variance in every measure (η² ≤ .007), and education essentially none. Gender reaches significance among lodging travellers but explains under 4% of variance (d ≈ 0.19). Neither gender nor education predicts which lodging style a traveller selects, with Cramér's V below 0.07 in both cases.

Against that backdrop, the within-person differences between the three measures stand out. The satisfaction → recommendation → revisit ladder is orders of magnitude stronger than any between-group effect tested here. It suggests that variation in these ratings lives inside the traveller's evaluation process, not in who the traveller is or where they stayed.

Limitations

  • Sample restriction. RQ1 and RQ3–RQ6 are confined to the 753 travellers with a lodging payment record — 26% of the full dataset. The results do not generalise to day-trippers.
  • Category collapsing. Lodging style was reduced from 11 categories to 5, and education from 8 levels to 4, because the original categories contained cells as small as n = 2. A different collapsing rule could produce different results, and the "Other" lodging category is conceptually heterogeneous.
  • Ceiling effect. All three measures cluster near the top of the 5-point scale (means 4.10–4.30), compressing the range in which group differences could appear and attenuating every effect size reported here.
  • Aggregation. Each rating is a per-traveller mean across visit areas, not a rating of the lodging itself. RQ1 therefore tests whether lodging style relates to overall trip evaluation, not to satisfaction with the accommodation.
  • Cross-sectional design. No causal inference is available from any of these tests; lodging style was self-selected, not assigned.
An alternative reading of RQ2. The descending ladder is presented as a satisfaction–intention gap, but it may be partly a scale artefact. The three items use different response anchors — satisfaction runs 매우 불만족 → 매우만족, while both intention items run 전혀 그렇지 않다 → 매우 그렇다. Respondents may simply use agreement scales more conservatively than evaluation scales, which would produce the same descending pattern with no attitudinal gap behind it. Distinguishing these would require a study design that varies the anchors; the present data cannot.
An alternative reading of the nulls. Four null results could mean these variables genuinely do not matter — or that the outcome measure is too coarse to detect them. Because ratings were averaged across all of a traveller's visit areas before analysis, an effect of lodging style on satisfaction with the lodging specifically would be diluted by every unrelated attraction, restaurant and shop in the same trip. A visit-area-level analysis restricted to type-24 rows would test that directly.
